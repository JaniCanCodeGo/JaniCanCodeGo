# The Forgotten Children of the 1994 Crime Bill — Business Plan

**Founder:** Jani
**Date:** July 2026
**Tagline:** *They sentenced our parents. Nobody counted us.*

> **How this plan was researched.** Every statistic below was gathered from primary
> sources (Bureau of Justice Statistics, Pew Charitable Trusts, The Sentencing Project,
> California Legislative Analyst's Office, Justice Policy Institute, Brennan Center,
> Urban Institute) and independently fact-checked before inclusion. Claims that could

## 1. Executive Summary

**The Forgotten Children of the 1994 Crime Bill** is a storytelling, education, and
healing platform for the people who were children during America's largest prison
build-up — the generation whose parents were incarcerated in the era of the Violent
Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, and who grew up in orphanages, foster
homes, group placements, and grandparents' living rooms as a result.

The founder is one of them. Her mother was sentenced federally for drug offenses under
a three-strikes provision. She passed through an orphanage, foster care, and kinship
care before returning to her mother — and went on to become a two-time college
graduate, a two-time homeowner, and a career public servant. The organization exists
to do three things:

1. **Count us.** Publish rigorously sourced statistics about the era's children —
   by race, gender, parental status, foster-care placement, recidivism, and
   economic outcome — and be honest about what the data does *not* show.
2. **Hear us.** A narrative podcast and a story-submission platform where the
   grown children of the build-up tell what happened in their own words.
3. **Heal us.** Plain-language education about trauma processing (EMDR and
   bilateral stimulation), with strict we-are-not-therapists guardrails, links to
   licensed care, and free self-regulation resources.

**Products at launch:** a 7-page website, a narrative podcast
(*The Forgotten Children*), and a story archive that grows into the organization's
long-term asset — the record nobody else kept.

## 2. The Problem (the verified version)

- Between 1990 and 2000 the U.S. prison population grew from **773,919 to
  1,381,892** — a 79% increase in one decade — peaking at **1,613,740 in 2009**
  (Bureau of Justice Statistics). By 2008, **1 in 100 American adults** was behind
  bars (Pew).
- The 1994 crime bill did not start that build-up, and we say so. What it did:
  created a **federal three-strikes provision**, authorized **$12.5 billion** in
  prison-construction grants tied to truth-in-sentencing requirements, and by 1999
  **42 states** had truth-in-sentencing laws — 9 adopted for the first time and 21
  amended during 1995–99 to qualify for the federal money (Urban Institute,
  Brennan Center).
- The build-up was not evenly distributed. In 2000, Black men were imprisoned at
  **3,457 per 100,000** versus 449 per 100,000 for white men (BJS). A Black boy
  born in 2001 faced a **1 in 3** projected lifetime chance of prison (BJS/Bonczar
  2003). In California, African Americans were **44.7% of third-strikers** while
  6.5% of the state population (Justice Policy Institute).
- Women were the fastest-growing population: incarcerated women rose **more than
  700%** from 1980 (26,378) to the 2019 peak (222,455), and **62% of women in
  state prison have a minor child** (The Sentencing Project).
- The children absorbed it. By 2010, **1 in 28 American children** had a parent
  behind bars — **1 in 9 Black children** (Pew, *Collateral Costs*). Family income
  fell **22%** while a father was incarcerated; formerly incarcerated people earn
  **40% less** annually (Pew).
- And then the cycle: **83% of released state prisoners were rearrested within
  9 years** (BJS 2005-cohort study).

**The gap this organization fills:** those children were never systematically
counted, followed, or heard. There is rich data on prisoners and thin, scattered
data on their children — and almost no platform where that generation, now adults
in their 30s–50s, tells its own story with the numbers done honestly.

## 3. Mission, Vision, Values

**Mission.** Make the children of the 1994-era prison build-up visible — in data,
in story, and in healing.

**Vision.** A public archive and audience large enough that no future sentencing
debate can ignore what mass incarceration does to the prisoner's children.

**Values.**
- *Accuracy over virality.* No unsourced statistic, ever. Where a popular number is
  a myth, we say so out loud.
- *Story ownership.* Contributors control their stories — anonymity on request,
  consent before publication, right to withdraw.
- *Healing without pretending.* We are not clinicians. We educate, disclaim, and
  refer to licensed care.
- *No pity.* The audience is not "at-risk statistics." They are survivors of policy.

## 4. Programs & Products

### 4.1 The website (launch asset)
Seven pages: **Home · The Bill · The Numbers · My Story · Healing · Podcast · Your
Story.** Bold protest-poster visual identity (primary colors, Impact-style display
type). The Numbers page is the credibility engine: charts built only from
`research/SOURCES.md`, each with its source named on the chart, plus a "what the
data does not show" section that openly addresses causation limits and data gaps.

### 4.2 The podcast (audience engine)
*The Forgotten Children* — narrative episodes pairing one sourced statistic with one
lived story (format and season one in `podcast/`). Distribution: all major podcast
platforms; episodes cross-post to the website. Cross-promotion with the founder's
existing show, *Clear Enough To Lead* (CETL), which shares production
infrastructure.

### 4.3 The story archive (long-term moat)
Story submissions arrive by email from the Your Story page, are consent-managed,
and are curated into (a) podcast listener-story episodes, (b) an anthology-ready
written archive, (c) anonymized aggregate themes that become original,
citable insight about this population — the data nobody collected.

### 4.4 Healing resources (service arm)
The Healing page: what EMDR is (with its actual evidence status — WHO and VA/DoD
recommend it for PTSD; APA conditionally recommends it), what bilateral stimulation
is, a link to a free browser-based bilateral-stimulation tool, and a positive-
cognitions library drawn from standard EMDR practice. Hard guardrails: prominent
not-therapists disclaimer, no trauma-reprocessing instructions, explicit "work with
a licensed EMDR clinician" referrals (EMDRIA directory).

## 5. Audience & Market

**Primary:** adults (roughly 30–55) who experienced parental incarceration as
children during the late-1980s–2000s build-up. Sizing signal: by 2010 there were
2.7 million children with a parent currently behind bars (Pew) and far more who had
ever experienced it — today those cohorts are adults.

**Secondary:** kinship and foster caregivers; social workers, educators, CASA
volunteers, reentry professionals; justice-reform audiences (listeners of
*Ear Hustle*, *70 Million*); researchers and journalists needing honest sourcing.

**Positioning.** Justice-media tells the prisoner's story; child-welfare media
tells the caseworker's story. Nobody owns the grown child's first-person story
told *with verified data*. That intersection — lived authority plus sourcing
discipline — is the moat, and it cannot be copied by anyone who didn't live it.

## 6. Revenue & Funding Model

Phased, deliberately modest, mission-first:

- **Phase 1 (Year 1) — Build the audience. Cost floor, not revenue.**
  Podcast + website + story pipeline. Costs: hosting, recording gear (shared with
  CETL), domain — under $2K. Funded personally.
- **Phase 2 (Year 2) — Community funding.** Listener support (Patreon/Supercast
  tier), live storytelling events, anthology pre-orders. Target: cover production
  costs plus modest stipend ($10–25K).
- **Phase 3 (Year 2–3) — Institutional funding.** Formalize as a nonprofit or
  fiscally sponsored project; approach funders active in this exact space
  (Annie E. Casey Foundation, public-safety reform funders, oral-history and
  documentary grants). The story archive and the sourced-statistics practice are
  the grant-readiness assets.
- **Explicitly not:** monetizing contributors' trauma. Story submissions are never
  paywalled or sold; contributors are paid where budget allows, never charged.

## 7. Operations

- **Founder-led**, part-time, alongside existing career and CETL commitments.
- **Production stack:** shared with CETL (recording, editing, hosting); website is
  a single static HTML file — no CMS, no server, near-zero cost, hostable on any
  static host (GitHub Pages, Netlify).
- **Story intake:** email to the address on the Your Story page → consent
  conversation → tagged archive. Volume triggers a proper form + database later.
- **Editorial standard:** nothing publishes with a number in it unless the number
  is in `research/SOURCES.md`.

## 8. Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| **Emotional load on founder** (the content is her own trauma) | Pacing; a personal clinician relationship; the right to skip topics; co-hosts for heavy episodes |
| **Statistics challenged publicly** | The sourcing discipline *is* the defense: every number cited to a primary source, data gaps disclosed before critics find them |
| **Mental-health liability** (Healing page) | No treatment instructions; not-therapists disclaimer at top of page; resource-installation framing only; referrals to EMDRIA-listed clinicians |
| **Contributor regret / privacy harm** | Consent process, anonymity by default option, right to withdraw a story at any time |
| **Founder time split with CETL and career** | Biweekly cadence; shared infrastructure; season-based production (batch recording) |
| **Political pile-ons** (the bill is a partisan football) | Stay in the lane: children's outcomes, verified data, no candidate endorsements |

## 9. Milestones & Metrics

**First 90 days:** website live · podcast trailer + 3 episodes banked · story
inbox open · 10 story submissions.
**Year 1:** 10-episode season complete · 100 story submissions · 5,000
downloads/month · one earned-media citation of The Numbers page.
**Metrics that matter:** story submissions (the archive is the point), podcast
completion rate, newsletter/email list growth, citations of the statistics page,
and — above all — contributor-reported experience of the process.

## 10. Relationship to CETL

Separate mission, shared founder and production infrastructure. CETL (teaching +
leadership podcast) and Forgotten Children cross-link and cross-promote; the
Forgotten Children website links back to the CETL page, and CETL's audience
provides the first listeners. Boundaries: separate brands, separate inboxes,
separate editorial standards documents.
