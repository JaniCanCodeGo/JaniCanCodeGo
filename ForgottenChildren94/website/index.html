Forgotten

The children of America’s biggest prison build-up are grown now — and telling the story.

They sentenced our parents. Courtrooms, case files, county vans. We were in the hallway, holding somebody’s hand.
Nobody counted us. No agency kept our ledger. So we are counting ourselves — with sources.

In 1994, Congress passed the largest crime bill in American history. The prisons filled. The sentences stretched. And more than a million children — the prisoners’ children — were handed to grandmothers, foster homes, group placements, and orphanage intake lines, with a prediction stapled to each file: this one ends up like the parent.

This project exists to get three things right. Count us — with statistics that are sourced, not viral. Hear us — through a podcast and a story archive in our own words. Heal us — with honest information about trauma work, and none of the fake kind.

1994

The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act — what it did, what it didn’t, and what it paid for.

Signed September 13, 1994. Roughly $30 billion authorized — the largest crime bill in U.S. history. This page tells it straight, because our credibility is the whole point.

What the bill did

Federal three strikes

Created 18 U.S.C. §3559(c): mandatory life in federal court for a third serious violent felony — or two violent felonies plus a serious drug offense.

Source: CRS R41461; DOJ Justice Manual

Prison money

Authorized $12.5 billion in state prison-building grants — about half conditioned on “truth in sentencing” laws requiring violent offenders to serve 85% of their sentences. (~$3B was ultimately appropriated, funding ~50,000 beds.)

Source: Brennan Center; Council on Criminal Justice

100,000 police

The COPS program funded community-policing hires nationwide — $8.8 billion up front.

Source: DOJ; Brennan Center

~60 new death-penalty offenses

A major expansion of the federal death penalty.

Source: FactCheck.org

Killed college in prison

Banned Pell grants for prisoners. Prison college programs collapsed from ~772 to 8 by 1997. (Restored in 2023.)

Source: Prison Policy Initiative; Vera Institute

And more

10-year assault weapons ban (expired 2004), the Violence Against Women Act, dozens of new federal offenses.

Source: FactCheck.org; Congress.gov

Three strikes: the honest timeline

The 1994 federal bill did not invent three-strikes laws. They were state laws, the wave started before the bill, and habitual-offender laws are a century old. What the federal bill did was join the wave, and then pay states to make sentences longer. Here is the record:

  • 1907–1926 New York’s repeat-felon law, then the Baumes Laws — mandatory life on a fourth felony. By 1945, more than twenty states had repeat-felony life laws.

    Source: Georgetown Am. Crim. L. Rev.; Britannica

  • 1984 The first state truth-in-sentencing laws appear — a decade before the federal bill.

    Source: BJS NCJ 170032

  • 1993 Washington State voters pass Initiative 593 — the first modern “three strikes, you’re out” law — with roughly three-quarters of the vote.

    Source: HistoryLink; State v. Thorne

  • Mar 1994 California enacts AB 971, its own three-strikes law — six months before the federal bill. Voters lock it in that November (Prop 184, 72%).

    Source: California LAO

  • Sep 1994 The federal crime bill is signed: federal three strikes (§3559(c)) plus prison grants tied to the 85% time-served standard.

    Source: P.L. 103-322; GAO GGD-98-42

  • 1993–95 Twenty-four states plus the federal government enact three-strikes laws, layered on top of existing repeat-offender enhancements. South Carolina makes it 25 states in 1996.

    Source: NIJ NCJ 165369 (Clark, Austin & Henry, 1997)

  • 1995–99 Chasing the federal money: 9 states adopt truth-in-sentencing for the first time; 21 more amend existing laws to hit the federal 85% standard.

    Source: Urban Institute (2002)

How the bill enhanced sentences where three-strikes laws already existed

The mechanism wasn’t commands — it was money. The bill’s VOI/TIS grants offered prison-construction funding to states that made violent offenders serve at least 85% of their sentences. When the GAO surveyed the 27 states taking that money in 1998: 4 states said the grants were a major factor in passing truth-in-sentencing laws, 11 said a partial factor, and 12 said no factor — their laws or momentum predated the bill. Oklahoma raised its proposed time-served requirement from 75% to 85% specifically to qualify.

So in states that already had habitual-offender or three-strikes laws, the federal bill’s effect was to lengthen the time actually served and to bankroll the beds that made longer sentences feasible — an accelerant poured on a fire the states had already lit.

Sources: GAO GGD-98-42 (1998); Urban Institute (2002); Brennan Center

Did the bill cause mass incarceration? The honest answer: no — and it didn’t have to.

Incarceration had already roughly quintupled since the early 1970s before the bill passed, and about 87% of prisoners are held by states, not the federal government. Brookings found state prison growth actually slowed after 1994. The Brennan Center calls the bill “not the root cause of mass incarceration” — but the most high-profile legislation ever to expand it. The ACLU’s counterpoint: the $12.5 billion and the 85% rule fed and entrenched the machine even if they didn’t build it. No credible count exists of “people imprisoned because of the 1994 bill,” and we will never invent one. What the bill unambiguously did: put a federal three-strikes law on the books, pay for the beds, stretch the time served, and take college out of prison for a generation.

Sources: National Academies; Brookings (2020); Brennan Center; Council on Criminal Justice; ACLU

The federal three-strikes provision itself

It was used rarely: through October 1998 there had been 59 federal three-strikes prosecution requests and 35 convictions, and no comprehensive running total has ever been published since. The heavier federal driver of life sentences for drug cases was an older law — 21 U.S.C. §841/§851 (1986/88), under which two prior drug felonies meant mandatory life without parole until the First Step Act softened it in 2018. As of January 2018, 1,545 people were serving federal life without parole for drug offenses.

Sources: Sentencing Project (1998); USSC §851 report (2018); ACLU “A Living Death” / BOP data

Prison didn’t just take parents — it took their earning power, then handed the bill to their children. Income drops while a parent is inside, stays lower after release, and the children start adulthood from behind. That is how a sentence becomes generational.

What the data does NOT show — read this before quoting us

  • No one can count “people imprisoned because of the 1994 bill.” No such estimate exists. The build-up began in the 1970s; ~87% of prisoners are in state systems; the bill accelerated, funded, and lengthened — we will not pretend to a precision the data doesn’t have.
  • “70% of prisoners’ children end up in prison” is an urban legend. Debunked in full (UConn IMRP, 2015). The defensible finding is ~2× raw risk of antisocial behavior — odds ratio 1.4 with controls.
  • “6× more likely to be incarcerated” is unsupported. Same review.
  • Different denominators are not interchangeable. “1 in 9 Black children” (Pew 2010, prison+jail) is not “1 in 15” (BJS 2007, prison only) is not “1 in 4” (Wildeman, ever-by-age-14). We label every frame.
  • Foster-care counts of prisoners’ children are undercounts — the systems weren’t built to see these children, which is the point of this whole project.

The Odds

My mother got a federal sentence. I got a prediction.

My mother was sentenced in federal court for drug offenses under a three-strikes provision — the machinery this site documents, applied to one woman, with one child in the hallway.

What followed for me was the standard route for the forgotten children, every stop of it: an orphanage, where I learned what an intake line is. Foster care, where I learned what a case file is. My grandmother’s keeping, where I learned — for the first time — what belonging to someone is. And then back with my mother, which nobody tells you is not an ending but a second beginning, with a stranger you love.

Then came the part that lasted the rest of my childhood: fighting a system whose own numbers said I would end up where she was. Teachers who expected less. Assumptions that arrived before I did. A file that traveled ahead of me into every room.

What the file predicted vs. what happened

The published odds for children with pieces of my file

  • Children of incarcerated parents: about 2× the raw risk of antisocial behavior Murray et al., 2012
  • Children of incarcerated fathers: 23% suspended or expelled vs 4% of other kids Pew, 2010
  • Family income: −22% while a parent is inside Pew, 2010
  • Youth who aged out of foster care: 36% homeless by 26; only 8% earn any college degree; half of those working earn ≤ $9,000/yr at 26 Midwest Study (Chapin Hall)
  • Aged-out young men: 57% arrested by 19 (vs 20% nationally) Midwest Study

What actually happened

  • Two college degrees. The Midwest Study says 8% of former foster youth hold any degree by 26 — roughly 1-in-12 odds. Nobody publishes odds for doing it twice.
  • Two homes owned. There is no published homeownership statistic for this population — the data assumes we don’t get here.
  • A career in public service — inside the kind of institution that once had custody of me. I didn’t escape the system. I walked back in with keys.
  • No cell. No case number of my own. The prediction was wrong.

An honest note about these odds

This is not an actuarial model of a person — no dataset can compute one child, and anyone who claims otherwise is selling something. These are published cohort statistics for groups whose files overlapped with mine (children of incarcerated parents; foster youth — noting the Midwest figures track youth who aged out, where I was ultimately reunified). They are here to show the direction and the weight of the prediction I lived under — not to claim false precision. The full citations are on The Numbers.

I am not the exception that excuses the system. I am the evidence of what every one of those children could have done with one grandmother, one chance, and one refusal. The statistics were never about our potential. They were about what was done to us.

Unbound

The trauma was real. So is the way out. Here’s what helped me — with every guardrail attached.

Read this first — we are not therapists

Nobody on this project is a therapist, psychotherapist, or clinician of any kind. Nothing on this page is treatment, and none of it replaces licensed care. In particular: do not attempt full EMDR trauma reprocessing on yourself. Clinicians and the peer-reviewed literature are clear that self-administered trauma reprocessing risks flooding, dissociation, and re-traumatization. What we describe for self-use below is the gentle, preparation-phase practice only — strengthening positive states. If you carry trauma like ours, please work with a licensed EMDR clinician: find one through EMDRIA’s directory. If you are in crisis in the U.S., call or text 988.

What EMDR is

EMDR — Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing — is a structured, eight-phase psychotherapy for traumatic memories. In it, a trained therapist has you briefly hold a memory in mind while guiding bilateral stimulation: alternating left-right input such as side-to-side eye movements, alternating tones, or taps. Done with a clinician, it helps stuck memories get processed so they stop running your nervous system.

Its evidence status, stated precisely: the World Health Organization recommends EMDR for PTSD in adults and children (2013); the VA/DoD recommend it among trauma-focused psychotherapies; the American Psychological Association conditionally recommends it (2017). It is a real, researched treatment — for a therapist’s office.

Sources: WHO (2013); VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline; APA PTSD Guideline (2017)

What we do recommend trying: positive cognitions

Inside EMDR there is a gentle preparation practice — resource development and installation — that uses slow bilateral stimulation while you hold a positive cognition: a true, strengthening statement about yourself. This calming, resourcing use is the one thing appropriate to do on your own, and it’s what I use myself: pick a cognition, start the slow stimulation, and let the statement settle in. If anything distressing comes up, stop and reach for a licensed clinician — that’s the boundary.
