# Episode 1 — "The Statistics Said I'd Be Her"

**Type:** Host solo narrative (origin episode)
**Target length:** 40 minutes
**Note:** Bracketed stage directions are for the host. All statistics cited in this
script must be read against `research/SOURCES.md` before recording; speak the source
name aloud with each number.

---

## COLD OPEN

[No music. Close mic.]

**HOST:** The first time I understood what a statistic was, I *was* one.

I was a child standing in an intake line at an orphanage, and somewhere in a filing
cabinet there was a number that described me: minor child, mother incarcerated,
federal sentence, no father in the home. That number had a prediction attached to it.
The prediction was that I would end up exactly where she was.

This show is about that prediction — who made it, who it came true for, and what it
took for it to be wrong about me.

[Beat. Music in — low, single instrument.]

**HOST:** My name is Jani. This is *The Forgotten Children*. They sentenced our
parents. Nobody counted us. So we're counting ourselves.

---

## SEGMENT 1 — THE NUMBER

**HOST:** Every episode, I give you one number and tell you exactly where it comes
from — because the children of prisoners have had round numbers thrown at us our
whole lives, and most of them were never true.

Today's number: [READ FROM SOURCES.md — the Bureau of Justice Statistics count of
minor children with a parent in prison, with year and source spoken aloud.]

Behind that number: in 1994, Congress passed the Violent Crime Control and Law
Enforcement Act — the crime bill. Here's what I need you to hear, because this show
does not do myths: the crime bill did not invent three-strikes laws. Washington
State's voters passed one in 1993. California passed its own in 1994. Habitual
offender laws are older than all of us. What the federal bill did was three things:
it created a *federal* three-strikes provision — the kind my mother was sentenced
under — it put billions of dollars on the table for states to build prisons, and it
tied that money to keeping people locked up longer. It poured fuel on a fire that
was already burning. The full sourcing for all of that is on our website, on the
page called The Numbers.

And when the doors locked, the children — us — went somewhere. Grandmothers,
mostly. Foster care. Group homes. In my case, first, an orphanage.

---

## SEGMENT 2 — THE STORY

[Host narrative. Outline beats — expand conversationally on record:]

1. **Before.** What I remember of my mother before the sentence. She was not a
   monster; she was a woman with drug offenses in a decade that had decided drug
   offenses meant life. Say it plainly: she was sentenced federally under a
   three-strikes provision.

2. **The day it happened.** What a child understands in a courtroom, which is
   almost nothing and also everything.

3. **The orphanage.** What intake feels like. The smell, the paperwork, the first
   night. I was told orphanages barely existed anymore in America. Mine existed.

4. **Foster care.** The moves. Being a case file. The particular loneliness of
   being safe and unwanted at the same time.

5. **Grandmother's keeping.** Kinship care — the invisible workforce of this whole
   era. The grandmothers who absorbed a generation. What she gave me that no
   placement could: I belonged to someone.

6. **Back with my mother.** What reunification actually is — not a finish line,
   a second beginning with a stranger you love.

7. **Fighting the system's math.** The teachers who expected less. The assumptions.
   Knowing — actually knowing — that the statistics on kids like me pointed one
   direction, and deciding, without any evidence yet, that I was the error term.

8. **Now.** Two college degrees. Two homes I own. A career in government — inside
   the kind of institution that once had custody of me. Say why that matters:
   I didn't beat the system by escaping it. I beat it by walking back in with keys.

---

## SEGMENT 3 — WHAT CARRIED ME

**HOST:** Every episode ends here, because the research on kids like us has a word:
*protective factors.* The things that bend the odds back.

Mine, in order:
- One grandmother who never once treated me like a risk score.
- Institutions I could grab — school, then college, then a second degree.
- And much later, trauma work. I'll say this every time I mention it: I am not a
  therapist. But EMDR — done with a licensed clinician — is where my childhood
  finally stopped running my nervous system. On our website's Healing page there's
  an explanation of it, a link to a free bilateral-stimulation tool, and a list of
  positive cognitions. That page starts with a disclaimer, and I mean it: don't
  self-administer trauma reprocessing. Do talk to someone licensed.

**HOST:** If your parent was taken in that era — if you were raised by a
grandmother, a caseworker, a group home, or nobody — this show is yours, and I want

You can stay anonymous. You were counted wrong once; be counted right this time.
