## 1. What the 1994 Crime Bill actually did

September 13, 1994) — a ~$30.2 billion authorization.
[FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2016/04/bill-clinton-and-the-1994-crime-bill/);
[Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/103rd-congress/house-bill/3355)

| Provision | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Federal three strikes | 18 U.S.C. § 3559(c): mandatory life for a third serious violent felony (or two violent + one serious drug offense) | [CRS R41461](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41461); [DOJ Justice Manual CRM 1032](https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-1032-sentencing-enhancement-three-strikes-law) |

## 2. Three-strikes laws — the honest timeline

**The 1994 federal bill did not create three-strikes laws.** They were state laws,
and the wave began before the bill:

- Habitual-offender laws date back a century: New York's 1907 repeat-felon law
  and 1926 Baumes Laws (mandatory life on a fourth felony); by 1920–1945 more
  than twenty states had repeat-felony life laws.
  [Georgetown Am. Crim. L. Rev.](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/american-criminal-law-review/in-print/volume-56/nomos-and-nullification-a-coverian-view-of-new-yorks-habitual-offender-law-1926-to-1936/);
  [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Baumes-Laws);
  [The Sentencing Project](https://www.sentencingproject.org/reports/the-eugenic-origins-of-three-strikes-laws-how-habitual-offender-sentencing-laws-were-used-as-a-means-of-sterilization/)
- First modern "three strikes, you're out": **Washington State, Initiative 593,
  November 1993** (~75–77% of the vote) — ten months before the federal bill.
  [HistoryLink](https://www.historylink.org/File/11148)
- **California, 1994** — twice: AB 971 signed March 1994 (six months before the
  federal bill) and Proposition 184 (November 1994, 72%).
  [CA LAO](https://www.lao.ca.gov/2005/3_strikes/3_strikes_102005.htm)
- **Between 1993 and 1995, 24 states plus the federal government** enacted
  three-strikes laws (25 states with South Carolina, 1996) — layered on top of
  pre-existing repeat-offender enhancements.
  [NIJ NCJ 165369 (Clark, Austin & Henry 1997)](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/three-strikes-and-youre-out-review-state-legislation-research-brief)
  *(individual state-year attributions vary by source — MEDIUM confidence on the
  per-state list, HIGH on the count)*

**How the federal bill enhanced sentencing in states that already had such laws:**

- VOI/TIS grants conditioned roughly half the prison money on adopting the **85%
  time-served** truth-in-sentencing standard.
  [GAO GGD-98-42](https://www.gao.gov/products/ggd-98-42)
- **GAO (1998), surveying the 27 funded states:** the grants were a *major factor*
  in 4 states, a *partial factor* in 11, and *not a factor* in 12 (their laws or
  momentum predated the bill). Oklahoma raised its proposed time-served standard
  from 75% to 85% specifically to qualify for the money. Same source.
- **Urban Institute (2002):** 1995–1999, **9 states adopted TIS laws for the first
  time and 21 more amended existing laws** to meet the federal 85% standard;
  TIS laws themselves date to 1984 (BJS).
  [Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/60401/410470-The-Influences-of-Truth-in-Sentencing-Reforms-on-Changes-in-States-Sentencing-Practices-and-Prison-Populations.PDF);
  [BJS NCJ 170032](https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/pdf/tssp.pdf)

**Federal three-strikes usage (rare):**

- Through October 1998: **59 federal three-strikes prosecution requests, 35
  convictions** (MEDIUM confidence — verify against PDF before print).
  [Sentencing Project, "Three Strikes: Five Years Later"](https://static.prisonpolicy.org/scans/sp/3strikes.pdf)
- **No comprehensive current count of § 3559(c) sentences is published** (absence
  verified against USSC life-sentence reports 2015/2022 and CRS R41461).
- The heavier federal driver of drug life sentences was 21 U.S.C. § 841/§ 851
  (enacted 1986/88, *not* the 1994 bill): two prior drug felonies = mandatory life
  without parole until the First Step Act (2018) reduced it to 25 years.
  FY2016: 6,153 eligible, 757 filings, 243 subject at sentencing.
  [USSC § 851 report](https://www.ussc.gov/research/research-reports/application-and-impact-section-851-enhancements)
- **1,545 people were serving federal life without parole for drug offenses as of
  January 2018** (BOP data via ACLU); ACLU 2013 counted 3,278 nonviolent-offense
  LWOP prisoners (federal + 9 states), ~79% drug crimes.
  [ACLU, "A Living Death"](https://www.aclu.org/publications/living-death-life-without-parole-nonviolent-offenses)
- State usage was wildly uneven: by 2004 only California (42,322), Georgia (7,631)
  and Florida (1,628) had >400 striker-prisoners; 14 states had fewer than 100.
  [JPI 2004](https://justicepolicy.org/wp-content/uploads/justicepolicy/documents/04-09_rep_threestrikesnatl_ac.pdf.pdf)

## 3. Did the bill cause mass incarceration? (the honest answer)

- Incarceration had roughly **quintupled since the early 1970s** before the bill
  passed; ~87% of prisoners are in state systems.
  [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18613/chapter/4);
  [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/did-the-1994-crime-bill-cause-mass-incarceration/)
- Brookings (2020): state prison growth *slowed* from ~7%/yr before 1994 to under
  3%/yr after; the bill "probably contributed to" but did not cause mass
  incarceration. Same URL.
- Brennan Center: "not the root cause of mass incarceration," but the most
  high-profile legislation to increase the number of people behind bars.
  [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/complex-history-controversial-1994-crime-bill)
- Council on Criminal Justice (Sabol & Johnson): the sentencing provisions "mostly
  intensified a movement already underway in the states."
  [CCJ](https://counciloncj.foleon.com/reports/crime-bill/i-impacts-on-prison-populations/)
- Counterpoint (ACLU): the prison money and the 85% standard fed and entrenched
  punitive state policy even if they didn't originate it.
  [ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/news/smart-justice/how-1994-crime-bill-fed-mass-incarceration-crisis)
- **No credible count exists of "people imprisoned because of the 1994 bill."**
  Anyone offering one is guessing. We present the build-up numbers and the bill's
  mechanisms, and label the bill an accelerant and consolidator, not the sole cause.

## 12. EMDR & bilateral stimulation (Healing page)

- **EMDR** = Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, an eight-phase
  psychotherapy for traumatic memories (Shapiro).
  [EMDRIA, the eight phases](https://www.emdria.org/blog/the-eight-phases-of-emdr-therapy/)
- Endorsements — state precisely: **WHO (2013) recommends EMDR** for PTSD in adults
  and children ([WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/news/item/06-08-2013-who-releases-guidance-on-mental-health-care-after-trauma));
  **VA/DoD recommends** it among trauma-focused psychotherapies
  ([VA](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/txessentials/emdr_pro.asp));
  **APA (2017) conditionally recommends** it — do not overstate as "strongly"
  ([APA](https://www.apa.org/ptsd-guideline/treatments/eye-movement-reprocessing)).
- **Bilateral stimulation (BLS):** alternating left–right sensory input (eye
  movements, tones, taps) used during EMDR.
- **Resource Development and Installation (RDI):** the preparation-phase practice of
  strengthening positive states/cognitions with slow BLS — the *only* form of
  self-use we point people to.
  [Leeds RDI](https://emdraa.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/RDI-Leeds-Slides-EMDRAA.pdf)
- **Safety:** self-administered trauma reprocessing is explicitly not recommended
  (risk of flooding, dissociation, re-traumatization).
  [Peer-reviewed commentary, PMC7576659](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7576659/)
  Referral: [EMDRIA Find a Therapist](https://www.emdria.org/find-an-emdr-therapist/)
- **Tool: [bilateralstimulation.io](https://www.bilateralstimulation.io/)** —
  free basic tier, no signup, browser-based (works in phone browsers; iOS 14.5+/
  Android Chrome), visual/audio/tactile modes, used by 40,000+ EMDR therapists.
  **Note:** no native app store app was found for this site; the turquoise-dot
  phone app the founder recalls may be a different product (e.g., "EMDR Tappers") —
  confirm the exact app on her phone before publicly naming it.

  [Arizona Trauma Institute list](https://aztrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/EMDR-Neg-Pos-Cog-List.pdf);

## Myths & Unsupported Claims — never publish these

1. **"70% of children of incarcerated parents will end up in prison."** Urban
   legend with no empirical basis.
   [UConn IMRP, "Seven Out of Ten? Not Even Close" (2015)](https://imrp.dpp.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3351/2021/09/March-2015-Seven-out-of-ten.pdf)
2. **"Children of incarcerated parents are 6× more likely to be incarcerated."**
   Unsupported (same UConn paper). Use Murray/Farrington/Sekol instead (~2× raw,
   OR 1.4 controlled).
3. **"70% of foster children have had an incarcerated parent."** No primary source
   found. Do not use.
4. **Any count of "people imprisoned because of the 1994 crime bill."** No such
   estimate exists; describe mechanisms and label the bill an accelerant.
5. **Mixed denominators.** 1-in-9 Black children (Pew, prison+jail, 2010) ≠ 1-in-15
   (BJS, prison only, 2007) ≠ 1-in-4 (Wildeman, ever-by-14, 1990 cohort). Never mix
   these in one chart; always label the frame.
6. **"20% of foster youth become homeless the day they age out."** Advocacy figure
   with weak provenance; use the Midwest Study's 36%-by-26 instead.
